# Managing Hypertension: The DASH Diet

![](https://www.thecardiologyadvisor.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2024/07/Priya-1-Square-1-150x150.jpeg)
![Food-products-representing-the-DASH-diet.](https://www.thecardiologyadvisor.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2025/08/GettyImages-2151229234-768x420.jpg)

*Our fact sheets aim to provide clinicians with easy-to-read resources to share with patients and their caregivers.*

High blood pressure, or hypertension, is a major contributor to many cardiovascular diseases, such as heart failure, stroke, and heart attack. It can also increase the risk of mortality. In the United States, approximately 49.64% of the population (roughly 115 million Americans), has hypertension.1 Of those with the condition, only about 39.64% of them are considered to have well-controlled [hypertension](https://www.thecardiologyadvisor.com/news/concurrent-hypertension-type-2-diabetes-heightens-mortality-risks/) (systolic blood pressure of <140 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure of <90 mm Hg). Therefore, it is important to discuss lifestyle modifications that can be implemented to help control high blood pressure. One of these modifications is making changes to one’s diet, such as incorporating the Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet.2

## What is the DASH Diet?

The DASH diet is a suggested diet by the American Heart Association to help lower blood pressure. The diet emphasizes foods that limit sodium and saturated fats. They are rich in minerals such as calcium, magnesium, and potassium.3 These include foods such as fruits, vegetables, lean meats (eg, poultry and fish), whole grains, and fat-free dairy products. Under the DASH diet, sodium is limited to 2300 mg a day, equivalent to a teaspoon of salt. A lower sodium version of the DASH diet limits daily sodium consumption even further to 1500 mg a day. The goal is to work on lowering sodium, as it is linked to significant increases in blood pressure.4

One study looked into the effects of the modified DASH diet on blood pressure in hypertensive and prehypertensive adults through a meta-analysis of previous studies and found remarkable results. They found that the diet resulted in decreases in mean systolic blood pressure by -3.36 mm Hg (95% CI, -5.58 to -0.94 mm Hg; *P* =.006) and mean diastolic blood pressure by -2.07 mm Hg (95% CI, -3.68 to -0.46 mm Hg; *P* =.01). They also found that there were greater observed decreases in systolic and diastolic blood pressure when the baseline blood pressure was higher before beginning the diet.5

## DASH Diet Servings

The suggested servings under the DASH diet vary based on different factors. These include factors such as the following: age, sex, activity level, and desire to lose weight. They all play roles in determining daily calories, which then determine the servings of each food group under the diet.

For example, if the patient is a 20-year-old woman who is often sedentary and does not want to lose weight, she would need about 2000 calories daily.  She would then need about 6 to 8 servings of grains, 4 to 5 servings of fruit, 4 to 5 servings of vegetables, 2 to 3 servings of fat-free/low-fat dairy products, 2 to 3 servings of fats/oils, and greater than 6 servings of lean meats daily, and about 3 to 4 servings of nuts, seeds, and legumes weekly. It is important to include each element of the DASH diet into one’s daily food goals as each component brings its own benefit. For example, fruit and vegetables are great sources of minerals such as potassium and magnesium. Dairy products act as strong sources of protein and calcium. Whole grains give energy and fiber.6

## DASH Diet Disadvantages

Like any other lifestyle change, there are some disadvantages that can make it difficult to implement. For example, it can be more time-consuming, as cooking with fresh items takes more time than purchasing ready-made or take-out meals. Moreover, it can also be more expensive, as often fresh foods are more expensive than their canned or processed counterparts. However, there are several ways to help with these issues. For example, meal prepping and planning weekly meals can help take away the daily time spent cooking. Moreover, shopping at farmer’s markets and buying fruits and vegetables in bulk can help reduce the cost associated with eating fresh.8

## Patient Frequently-Asked Questions (FAQs)

### Can following the DASH diet help with weight loss?

A: Because the diet incorporates low-sodium, nutrient-rich foods, it can help promote weight loss. This change in diet, along with exercise and focusing on calorie deficit, can support patients with their weight loss goals.

### Can I still eat at restaurants on the DASH diet?

A: Yes, it is still possible to eat at restaurants when under the DASH diet. However, many restaurant dishes are high in sodium, so certain changes can be made to still stay on the diet. For example, you can order vegetables on the side instead of carbohydrate-based sides. You can also make changes like opting for grilled or baked foods instead of fried.

### How can I start making changes to adhere to the DASH diet?

A: It is important to make gradual changes and incorporate them into your lifestyle. You can start by decreasing the amount of salt added to meals. Incremental increases of vegetable and fruit servings into meals can also promote adherence.

### How can I measure if the DASH diet is working?

A: You can measure your blood pressure daily in a log book to track changes over time. You can also note changes in your energy and mood. Other changes may also be seen in bloodwork over time; the diet consists of nutrient-rich foods that can help decrease cholesterol and LDL-C levels.

### Can I drink alcohol when on the DASH diet?

A: Yes, you can drink alcohol in moderation when on the DASH [diet](https://www.thecardiologyadvisor.com/features/weight-inclusivity-in-health-care/). Drinking in moderation for men is 2 drinks a day and for women is 1 drink a day. It is important to limit alcohol consumption, as high alcohol intake can lead to increases in blood pressure.
