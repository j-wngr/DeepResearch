# DASH and Mediterranean diets outperform sodium restriction in lowering blood pressure

* JUNE 9, 2025

* 9.779

A clinical trial has compared the effect of a Mediterranean diet or a sodium-restricted DASH diet on blood pressure in people with stage 1 hypertension. The results show that a healthy diet is more effective than sodium restriction alone for managing hypertension.  
   

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND MECHANISMS

High blood pressure is a chronic physiological condition that increases the risk of heart attack, stroke, and kidney damage, and is one of the leading risk factors for mortality. Sodium restriction has been one of the main medical recommendations for lowering hypertension. However, blood pressure regulation involves genetic, hemodynamic, neuroendocrine, inflammatory, metabolic, environmental, and other factors. This means that there are different ways to reduce hypertension, and the recommendation to reduce sodium is a very partial approach. The DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) has been created specifically for the management of hypertension. It is a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, legumes, and low-fat dairy products, characterized by its low sodium and fat content, particularly saturated fats. Several controlled studies conducted in recent years have evaluated its effect on hypertension.   
   

## STUDY

A randomized controlled clinical trial (Filippou et al., 2023) compared the effectiveness of the DASH diet and the Mediterranean diet, both with sodium restriction, versus sodium restriction alone. The study included 240 adults up to 65 years of age with normal-high blood pressure or grade 1 hypertension without medication, who were assigned to 4 groups. The sodium restriction group, the Mediterranean diet group, and the DASH diet group all received training to limit their daily sodium intake to less than 2,000 mg (5 grams of salt), menus tailored to their caloric needs, and training by a clinical dietitian. The control group only received general advice in a brief session and follow-up measurements. Participants were not required to change their physical activity or reduce their body weight. Assessments included anthropometric measurements, dietary and physical activity questionnaires, and blood pressure measurements in the clinic and by 24-hour ambulatory monitoring. Fasting blood and urine samples were also collected. The primary outcome was change in systolic blood pressure; secondary outcomes included changes in diastolic blood pressure, changes in ambulatory monitoring, and anthropometric parameters. Dietary adherence was monitored using urinary biomarkers.  
   

## MAIN RESULTS

The results showed large reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the intervention groups compared to the control group. The Mediterranean diet with sodium restriction achieved the greatest reduction in systolic pressure, followed by the DASH diet, and finally salt restriction alone. For diastolic pressure, the Mediterranean and DASH diets achieved greater reductions than salt restriction, with no significant differences between them. In ambulatory monitoring, both diets reduced average systolic blood pressure similarly and significantly. In addition, the Mediterranean diet was found to achieve greater reductions in average diastolic blood pressure during the day and night, although not always with significant differences compared to the DASH diet. These findings were consistent in subgroups according to initial blood pressure, confirming the superiority of combined diets over restriction alone. Adherence was high, and no changes in body weight or physical activity were observed, supporting that improvements in blood pressure were mainly derived from dietary modifications.  
   

## CONCLUSION AND CLINICAL RELEVANCE

The study reflects that the effect of diet on blood pressure is not reducible to sodium intake, given that the groups that followed low-sodium DASH and Mediterranean diets reduced their blood pressure well above the sodium restriction group alone. However, as there were no DASH and Mediterranean diet groups without sodium restriction, the study does not allow us to fully clarify the role of sodium in a healthy diet. In other words, it is unclear whether sodium reduction or diet is more important, or whether a higher intake of blood pressure-modulating minerals such as potassium, magnesium, etc. is more important. However, the author of this study had previously published a systematic review of the effect of the DASH diet on hypertension, with €œparadoxical€ results if a linear relationship between sodium restriction and blood pressure reduction is assumed. Filippou found a greater decrease in blood pressure with diets with more moderate sodium restriction. This issue is therefore much more complex than has traditionally been assumed in medicine. When analyzing the dietary components of the DASH and Mediterranean diets, both are based on high consumption of vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and fruits, as well as a reduction in processed foods, red meat, and added sugars. The main difference between the two dietary patterns is that the DASH diet is generally based on a lower fat intake than the Mediterranean diet. Otherwise, they can be very similar dietary patterns, depending on the foods available in each region. Part of the reduction in blood pressure will simply come from the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant environment provided by this type of food. Simply reducing ultra-processed foods and sugars, increasing the consumption of fresh vegetables and some fats such as extra virgin olive oil, is already antihypertensive and brings us closer to a Mediterranean or DASH diet, [without the formal name of the dietary pattern being of greater relevance.](https://www.icns.es/noticia_dietas_antiinflamatorias_disminuyen_tension_arterial_riesgo_cardiometabolico) However, the reductions in this clinical trial are well above those shown in other studies, so the results should be interpreted in conjunction with the overall evidence from the various clinical trials and systematic reviews, which paint a more complex picture than is assumed.  
   
 Finally, it should be understood that the goal is not to reduce blood pressure per se, but to reduce mortality. Therefore, cardiometabolic health and cardiovascular risk in individuals go beyond restricting sodium and reducing blood pressure levels. We will leave this question for another article given the complexity of the topic and the conflicting scientific evidence on this issue. However, blood pressure depends on overall metabolic health, depending on factors such as microbiota, body composition, muscle mass, visceral fat mass, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, etc. Therefore, not everyone responds well to a simple dietary recommendation to follow a Mediterranean or similar pattern. If there is a poor initial response, a progressive clinical nutrition approach should be taken, targeting different metabolic pathways (improvement of the microbiota-inflammation-blood pressure axis, introduction of specific nutrients that act on different pathways, etc.).
